Law, Courts & Your Role

The legal system, the courts, your rights and your duties as a UK resident

Criminal vs Civil — The Big Distinction

Criminal Crimes against society

The state prosecutes a defendant who has broken the law. If guilty, the defendant is punished — fine, community service, prison.

  • Burden: the prosecution must prove guilt 'beyond reasonable doubt'
  • Examples: theft, assault, drug offences, drink-driving, murder
  • Brought by: the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) on behalf of the state
  • Possible outcomes: fine, community order, imprisonment, suspended sentence

Civil Disputes between people

One party (the claimant) sues another (the defendant) for damages or a remedy. The losing side may have to pay compensation but is not punished by the state.

  • Burden: 'on the balance of probabilities' (lower than criminal)
  • Examples: divorce, debt, contract disputes, personal injury, defamation, employment
  • Brought by: the claimant (the person bringing the case)
  • Possible outcomes: compensation (damages), injunction, court order
The one-sentence test Crime = wrong against society, prosecuted by the state, punishment. Civil = dispute between two parties, brought by one of them, compensation. Test questions often pose a scenario and ask which type it is.
The Court Hierarchy (England & Wales)
The flow Most crimes start in the Magistrates' Court. Serious crimes (murder, rape, robbery) go up to the Crown Court for a jury trial. Civil disputes start in the County Court; bigger civil cases in the High Court. Appeals go to the Court of Appeal, then ultimately the Supreme Court.
Juries
WhereThe Crown Court (and serious civil cases such as fraud or defamation)
Number of jurors12 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. 15 in Scotland.
EligibilityAged 18–75, on the electoral register, and a UK resident for at least 5 years since age 13. People with serious criminal convictions may be disqualified.
SelectionRandom selection from the electoral register. You receive a summons in the post.
Civic dutyJury service is compulsory unless you have a valid reason to be excused. Most people serve for about 10 working days.
VerdictEngland: ideally unanimous; majority verdicts allowed (e.g. 10–2) after deliberation. Scotland: simple majority of 15. Three Scottish verdicts: Guilty, Not Guilty, Not Proven.
Quick fact Scotland's legal system is separate from England's. The "Not Proven" verdict (a third option meaning 'we suspect but can't be sure') is unique to Scotland.
Police, Lawyers and Other Officials
PoliceIndependent of government. Each force has a Chief Constable (or Commissioner in London). Police & Crime Commissioners are elected by the public in England and Wales (since 2012).
SolicitorA lawyer you go to first — gives advice, prepares documents, deals with most legal matters. Can represent you in lower courts.
BarristerA specialist court advocate. Argues cases in higher courts. Usually instructed via a solicitor.
Magistrate / JPVolunteer, unpaid. Sits in the Magistrates' Court. Usually three lay magistrates hear minor cases.
JudgeTrained lawyer; presides over the Crown Court and higher courts. Decides points of law and (in non-jury cases) the verdict and sentence.
CoronerInvestigates unexpected, violent or unexplained deaths.
Your Rights — and How They Are Protected
RightWhat it means
EqualityThe Equality Act 2010 protects against discrimination on grounds of: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage / civil partnership, pregnancy, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation. These are the nine protected characteristics.
Free expressionFreedom of speech — you may criticise the government, the law, religion, public figures — subject to laws against incitement, defamation and hate speech.
Free associationRight to join a political party, trade union, faith group, or any lawful organisation.
Fair trialRight to be presumed innocent until proven guilty; right to legal representation; right to silence; right to a public hearing.
PrivacyProtected by the Human Rights Act 1998 and the Data Protection Act 2018 / UK GDPR.
VoteCitizens 18+ may vote in UK elections. Resident Commonwealth and Irish citizens can also vote in many UK elections.
Test fact — Equality Act 2010 The handbook expects you to know that the Equality Act protects certain characteristics. The key ones to remember: age, disability, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation. You don't need to recite all nine, but recognise them as protected.

Your Duties & Civic Responsibilities

Obey the law

Including obscure ones — ignorance of the law is not a defence. The law applies equally to everyone, including the monarch (in principle).

Pay taxes

Income tax (PAYE through your employer, or self-assessment if self-employed). National Insurance contributions. Council tax for local services. VAT (20% on most goods and services).

Tax year: 6 April → 5 April

Serve on a jury

If summoned, you must serve. Excuse yourself only with good reason (medical, conflict, etc.). Employers are required to release you for jury service.

Register to vote

You can register at gov.uk. Compulsory in the sense that you can be fined for refusing to provide details to your local council, although you don't have to actually vote.

Respect & tolerance

Treat others fairly regardless of their faith, race, gender or sexuality. The principles of mutual respect and tolerance are listed as fundamental British values.

Look after the environment

Recycle waste, follow planning rules, do not litter or dump rubbish (fly-tipping is a crime), respect public spaces and parks.

Help your community

Volunteering, school governorship, charity work, helping neighbours, becoming a magistrate (you can apply with no legal training), or standing for local council. The handbook treats these as expected, not optional, ways of being a good citizen.
